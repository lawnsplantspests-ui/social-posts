## 1. Where yellow jackets go in winter (post now, late Oct / early Nov)

**Images:** (1) queen coming out of the soil, March 21, 2026 (IMG_3861) · (2) Pest Control ad (IMG_4134)

**Caption:**
The yellow jacket nest in your yard won't survive the winter. The first hard frost finishes off the workers, and the nest is never reused.

What does survive is the new queens. Right now they're leaving the nest, mating, and tucking themselves into soil, rotting logs, leaf litter and wall voids to wait out the cold. This one came back up out of the dirt on a warm day in late March.

Each queen that makes it through the winter starts a brand-new nest in spring, by herself. That's why the cheapest yellow jacket problem is the one that never gets started. A spring perimeter treatment makes your house a poor place for her to pick.

## 2. One queen = one future nest (save for April)

**Images:** (1) yellow jacket queen on mulch, May 26, 2026 (IMG_4757) · (2) Pest Control ad (IMG_4134)

**Caption:**
See a big yellow jacket cruising slowly along your siding, mulch beds or porch in April and May? That's almost always a queen, and she's house hunting.

In spring there's no colony yet, just one insect choosing a soffit corner, a gap behind siding, or an old burrow in the lawn. By August that same spot can hold thousands of workers.

Keeping a perimeter treatment on through spring is what breaks the "nest in the same soffit every year" pattern.

## 3. Paper wasp or yellow jacket? (save for June)

**Images:** (1) paper wasps on a deck board, June 25, 2026 (IMG_5107)

**Caption:**
Paper wasp or yellow jacket? Here's a quick way to tell.

Paper wasps (pictured) are slimmer, with long legs that dangle below them when they fly. They build open, umbrella-shaped nests you can see the cells on, under deck rails, eaves and grills. Colonies are small and they're less aggressive.

Yellow jackets are stockier, fly fast and tuck their legs up, and hide their nests in the ground or inside walls. That's the one that gets defensive by August.
